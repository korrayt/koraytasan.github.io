Medusa’ya ne oldu?

— I —

The gods delight in telling what befalls mankind; yet of the causes of their own deeds they are seldom so generous.

And thus some stories reach us incomplete.

It is said that a man cut off a woman’s head.

It is said that a goddess wore that head upon her breast.

It is said that two children sprang from a severed neck.

And all these things may be true.

Only, they may not be the truth.

For truth is greater than the thing that happened.

— II —

Before Perseus sets out upon his journey, he comes to the temple of Athena.

The goddess commands him never to look directly upon the face of his enemy. The bronze shield she places in his hands has been polished so perfectly that even stone might behold its own face in it.

“Do not look,” says Athena.

“Look only upon the reflection.”

Perseus raises the shield and sees his own eyes.

He does not yet know:

Sometimes a man uses a mirror so that he need not look upon another.

And sometimes so that, for the first time, he may truly see.

In the shadowed part of the temple, a young man listens.

His name is Yusuf.

Athena did not give birth to him.

Indeed, men will later call Athena *Parthenos*, and say that she had never lain with any man.

That too is true.

But motherhood does not always begin in birth.

Athena takes Yusuf into her temple while he is still a child; she teaches him letters and numbers, the veins hidden within stone, the polishing of bronze, and how a war may be won before a sword is ever drawn.

She calls him son.

And for a long time Yusuf calls her mother.

When Athena has left the temple, Yusuf approaches Perseus.

“You are going to kill Medusa.”

Perseus is not surprised.

In the temples of gods, secrets often live longer than walls.

“Have you seen her?” Yusuf asks.

Perseus laughs.

“Whoever sees her turns to stone.”

Yusuf does not laugh.

“I have seen her.”

And this is the source of the sentence Perseus will speak many years later at a banquet:

*I found one who said that he had seen her.*

But at that banquet Perseus does not speak Yusuf’s name.

For some names are not forgotten.

They are hidden.

Yusuf is silent for a while.

Then he begins to describe Medusa.

Not the monster.

The woman.

He tells how her hair shone like gold beneath the sun. How men stopped speaking when she passed, though Medusa pretended not to notice.

How she tilted her head slightly when she laughed.

How her lips trembled when anger took her.

How she would sit barefoot upon the marble steps of the temple.

And how, once, Yusuf said to her:

“All men are looking at you.”

And Medusa answered:

“So are you.”

“What happened then?” asks Perseus.

Yusuf looks into the shining surface of the shield.

“Then the gods meddled with the beauty of mortals.”

He says no more.

— III —

When Perseus departs the following morning, Yusuf goes with him.

Athena has not given him permission.

Yusuf has not asked for it.

Long roads hasten friendship.

When two men sleep beside the same fire, drink from the same water, and can turn their backs upon an enemy because the other guards them, a bond older than blood begins to form.

Perseus knows how to fight.

Yusuf knows how to wait.

Perseus leaps forward.

Yusuf sees two steps beyond.

Each is the part the other lacks.

Mankind will later tell the story of these two men in other lands, in other ages, beneath other names.

For men change stories.

But the story of two companions who find one another is not so easily changed.

— IV —

Night has fallen when they reach the dwelling of the Gorgons.

Stheno sleeps.

Euryale sleeps.

And between them lies Medusa.

Perseus first looks into his shield.

Then his breath stops.

For what he sees in the mirror is not a monster.

It is a woman still hidden within monstrosity.

A face appearing among the serpents…

Weary.

Sorrowful.

Innocent.

And beautiful.

For a long while Perseus does not move.

Yusuf whispers:

“Now you too have seen love…”

Perseus gives no answer.

The answer to some sentences is love itself.

And love is often left unspoken.

Medusa opens her eyes.

Perseus instinctively withdraws behind the shield.

Yusuf does not.

He closes his eyes.

“Yusuf?”

Medusa’s voice rises from the darkness.

When a man has longed for a voice for years, hearing his own name spoken by it may feel like being touched.

Yusuf falls to his knees.

“I am here.”

Medusa smiles.

It is a bitter smile.

“Have you come to kill me?”

“No.”

“The man beside you has.”

Perseus speaks.

“I have.”

Silence.

“And now?”

Perseus looks upon her reflection in the shield.

“Now… I do not know.”

For the first time in many years, Medusa truly laughs.

An innocent smile.

That a monster feared by mankind should find amusement in the hesitation of the man sent to kill her would not have pleased the gods.

But that night the gods were not listening.

Or believed they were.

— V —

The three speak for days.

Perseus sees Medusa only through the shield.

Yusuf sits beside her with his eyes closed.

Sometimes Medusa lies between them.

And the strange thing is this:

For years Medusa has feared the gaze of men.

Now before her sit two men who cannot look at her.

And for the first time in her life, she is understood without being seen.

Perseus tells her Athena’s plan.

If he does not return with her head, he cannot escape the wrath of Polydectes.

And Athena desires Medusa’s death.

Medusa is silent for a long time.

Then she says:

“Then let us kill me.”

Yusuf rises.

“No.”

“What remains of me, Yusuf?”

“You.”

“Who can touch me?”

Yusuf extends his hand.

His eyes remain closed.

Medusa places her hand in his palm.

Yusuf does not turn to stone.

For the curse lies in sight.

Not in touch.

Medusa begins to weep.

Perhaps this is what Athena forgot.

To curse a human face is not enough to deprive a human being of love.

Men simply seek love too often in the eyes.

— VI —

The plan comes from Yusuf.

Perhaps Athena has taught him too well:

How a war may be won before the sword is drawn.

“Perseus will cut off Medusa’s head.”

Perseus stares at him.

“Is that the terrible part of the plan?”

“No,” says Yusuf.

“The terrible part comes after.”

He tells them the ancient rumours concerning the blood of the Gorgon.

Gorgon blood possesses two natures.

One extinguishes life.

The other summons life back.

Poison and remedy flow within the same body.

Death and life are two chambers of the same heart.

“What if the wrong side flows?” asks Perseus.

Without looking at Medusa, Yusuf smiles.

“Then one of us dies.”

Perseus understands what he means.

“No.”

Yusuf says nothing.

Medusa understands as well.

“No.”

Again Yusuf says nothing.

For once a man has chosen to sacrifice himself, the objections of others usually do no more than prolong the farewell.

— VII —

In the days that follow, Medusa becomes pregnant with two children.

The genealogies of the gods will lie about this.

They will write that Poseidon fathered both sons.

Temples will repeat it.

Poets will repeat it.

Priests will repeat it.

And like every lie repeated long enough, it will become myth for generations yet unborn.

But Medusa loves two men.

And bears two children.

Pegasos carries the blood of Perseus.

Khrysaor the blood of Yusuf.

That one womb should carry children of two fathers may seem strange to mortals.

But Medusa is no longer merely mortal.

Bodies cursed by gods need not obey the laws of gods.

— VIII —

Night falls again while the Gorgon sisters sleep.

This time Medusa truly sleeps.

Yusuf gives her a drink.

“Will it hurt?” asks Medusa.

Perseus cannot answer.

Yusuf says:

“You will not remember.”

Medusa turns toward the sound of Yusuf’s voice.

“And you?”

Yusuf smiles.

“I will remember.”

Perseus raises the shining shield.

He does not look upon Medusa’s face, but upon its reflection.

The curved blade in his hand catches the moonlight.

And what history will record comes to pass.

Perseus cuts off Medusa’s head.

From the severed neck two children emerge.

Pegasos rises into the heavens.

Khrysaor is born from the blood.

At that same instant two streams of blood begin to flow.

Yusuf stands upon the side of poison.

Perseus upon the side of life.

Perseus cries out.

Medusa’s body convulses.

Yusuf falls.

Perseus catches him.

Yusuf smiles.

“Athena taught me this.”

“What?”

“In war, sometimes the most precious piece must sacrifice itself.”

And he dies.

Athena’s son, by the very strategy Athena taught him, has defeated Athena for the first time—

by killing himself.

— IX —

Perseus carries Medusa’s head away.

Athena believes herself victorious.

She fixes the head upon her Aegis.

She shows it to her enemies.

She turns kings to stone.

She silences armies.

In temples men say:

“Medusa is dead.”

But Medusa sees.

She hears.

She remembers.

She cannot speak.

Years pass.

Men walk before her.

Some gaze in terror.

Some in wonder.

Some see only the power of Athena.

None knows that the eyes upon the Aegis are still alive.

The woman who was once punished because of her body has now been reduced to a face.

And even that face no longer belongs to her.

Athena carries her as a weapon.

And through the long years Medusa learns only one thing:

To wait.

— X —

Khrysaor grows.

He does not know his father’s name.

But he knows the story of his mother.

Perseus tells him of Yusuf.

He gives him the shining shield.

“Your father would have done this,” he says.

“Done what?”

“Known how to win before standing before a god.”

Khrysaor enters Athena’s temple.

When Athena sees him, she recognizes his mother’s eyes.

“Why have you come?”

Khrysaor smiles.

“To see you.”

“Do you challenge me?”

“No.”

What Athena hates is not challenge.

It is contempt.

Khrysaor knows this.

“My mother was more beautiful than you,” he says.

Athena’s face changes.

“What did you say?”

“Yusuf did not choose you either.”

And here the stone first cracks.

For the anger of gods often rises not from their strength, but from their wounds.

Athena raises the Aegis.

She turns Medusa’s face toward Khrysaor.

“Look upon your mother.”

Khrysaor raises the shield.

But then something else happens.

Medusa sees her son.

For the first time in years, a single tear slips from eyes that have stared without moving.

For the first time in years, Medusa chooses whom she will turn to stone.

Her gaze strikes the polished shield.

And Athena sees Medusa’s eyes upon its shining surface.

What could be more dangerous to a goddess than the reflection of her own act?

First her hands grow heavy.

Then her feet.

Marble walks inward through her flesh.

Athena tries to cry out.

Her voice becomes stone.

She tries to flee.

Her legs become stone.

She cannot tear her eyes from Medusa’s eyes reflected in the shield.

At last only Athena’s eyes remain.

And they too harden.

Athena becomes a statue in her own temple.

But she does not die.

She is conscious.

She sees.

She hears.

She cannot speak.

She enters the punishment she once gave Medusa.

Even gods sometimes understand justice only after they have endured it.

— XI —

The instant Athena turns to stone, the curse breaks.

The serpents in Medusa’s hair twist.

One by one they fall silent.

Their scales become gold.

Golden strands of hair.

Her teeth grow smaller.

Her skin softens.

And the severed head that has lived for years upon the Aegis remembers its body as blood remembers its own vessel.

Flesh finds bone.

The neck finds shoulders.

The shoulders find arms.

The arms find hands.

Medusa is not reborn.

She returns to herself.

When she looks into a mirror, she does not see the monster.

She sees the young woman who once sat within Athena’s temple.

But she is no longer that woman.

For a person may return to an old body.

No one returns to an old life.

Perseus grows old.

Then he dies.

Mortality is not his curse.

It is his nature.

Medusa buries him.

Pegasos circles for a long while above the grave of his father.

And Medusa learns what it is to lose one man without his being murdered,

and to wait for another though he is dead.

— XII —

And one day Medusa brings forth the small vessel she has kept hidden.

When Yusuf died, Perseus had preserved a few drops of the blood that restores life.

Medusa touches it to Yusuf’s lips.

The dead body breathes.

But time does not return.

Yusuf cannot return to the same world.

And so he needs another name.

Men come to know him as:

**Phidias.**

A sculptor who understands stone better than other men.

Of course he understands stone.

For throughout one life the woman he loved could not look upon him without turning him to stone.

And the woman who raised him is stone now.

— XIII —

Phidias stands before Athena.

The petrified goddess remains conscious.

She recognizes him.

Yusuf places his hand upon Athena’s cold cheek.

“Mother.”

Athena’s eyes cannot move.

But Yusuf knows she remains within.

“You raised me well.”

Then he begins his work.

Men believe Phidias creates the statue of Athena.

They are wrong.

He does not make the statue.

The statue already exists.

Around the petrified body of Athena, Phidias lays gold and ivory.

He makes her armour.

He makes her garments.

He makes her helmet.

He transforms her into the enormous image of a goddess.

And for centuries men say:

“Phidias’ Athena Parthenos.”

None knows that the true Athena stands imprisoned at the centre of the work.

One of the greatest statues in the history of art is not a statue at all.

It is a prison.

And all Phidias has done is make its door beautiful.

— XIV —

When the work is finished, Yusuf stands for a long time before Athena.

He looks upon the mother from whom he once lowered his eyes.

Athena looks back.

For the first time she cannot command him.

For the first time Yusuf does not ask permission to leave.

He turns his back.

He goes home.

Medusa is in the garden.

Sunlight falls upon her hair.

Its gold remains.

There are no serpents.

No shield.

No mirror.

Yusuf stops in the doorway.

Medusa looks at him.

Yusuf does not close his eyes as he once did.

Fear crosses Medusa’s face.

For some fears do not vanish merely because the curse is gone.

“Yusuf…”

Yusuf walks toward her.

And for the first time he looks directly into her eyes.

A moment passes.

Then another.

He does not turn to stone.

Nothing happens.

Medusa weeps.

Yusuf places his hand upon her face.

And for the first time in her life, Medusa is not afraid that a man is looking at her.

Men will later tell that Medusa was a monster.

They will tell that Perseus killed her.

They will tell that Athena carried her head as a weapon.

They will tell that Phidias made the statue of Athena.

None of these things is wholly false.

For myths do not often lie.

They merely decide which part of truth they will tell.

And sometimes, to change the story of a woman, there is no need to change what has been said about her.

One need only ask the question no one thought to ask:

Not—

**What did Medusa become?**

But—

**What happened to Medusa?**

— I —

Di hominum casus narrare amant; suorum autem factorum causas non pari semper liberalitate aperiunt.

Ita fit ut nonnullae fabulae mutilae ad nos perveniant.

Vir caput feminae amputavisse dicitur.

Dea illud caput pectori suo imposuisse dicitur.

Duo liberi e collo reciso nati esse dicuntur.

Haec omnia vera esse possunt.

Nec tamen ipsa veritas esse.

Maior enim est veritas quam id quod accidit.

— II —

Perseus, priusquam iter incipit, ad templum Athenae venit.

Dea iubet eum ne vultum hostis recta fronte aspiciat. Scutum aeneum, quod ei tradit, tam nitidum est ut saxum ipsum vultum suum in eo videre possit.

“Noli aspicere,” inquit Athena.

“Imaginem tantum aspice.”

Perseus scutum tollit atque oculos suos videt.

Nondum intellegit:

Interdum homo speculo utitur ne alterum aspiciat.

Interdum autem ut eum primum vere videat.

In obscuriore parte templi iuvenis audit.

Iosephus nomen ei est.

Athena eum non peperit.

Homines enim postea Athenam *Parthenon* vocabunt eamque cum nullo viro concubuisse narrabunt.

Id quoque verum est.

Sed maternitas non semper a partu incipit.

Athena Iosephum adhuc puerum in templum recipit; litteras numerosque docet, venas lapidum, aes poliendi artem, atque quomodo bellum ante strictum gladium vincatur.

Filium eum vocat.

Et Iosephus diu matrem eam appellat.

Athena templo egressa, Iosephus ad Perseum accedit.

“Medusam interfecturus es.”

Perseus non miratur.

In templis deorum secreta saepe diutius quam muri vivunt.

“Vidistine eam?” rogat Iosephus.

Perseus ridet.

“Qui eam videt, lapis fit.”

Iosephus non ridet.

“Ego vidi.”

Hinc illa sententia oritur quam Perseus multis annis post inter epulas dicturus est:

*Inveni qui se vidisse referret.*

Sed eo convivio nomen Iosephi Perseus non dicit.

Sunt enim nomina quae non oblivione pereunt.

Occultantur.

Iosephus paulisper tacet.

Deinde Medusam describere incipit.

Non monstrum.

Feminam.

Comas eius sub sole velut aurum fulgere narrat. Homines, cum illa praeteriret, sermonem intermisisse; Medusam autem quasi nihil animadvertisset se gessisse.

Cum rideret, caput paulum inclinasse.

Cum irasceretur, labra tremuisse.

In marmoreis templi gradibus nudis pedibus sedere solitam.

Atque semel Iosephum dixisse:

“Omnes viri te spectant.”

Medusam respondisse:

“Tu quoque spectas.”

“Quid deinde?” rogat Perseus.

Iosephus nitidam scuti faciem intuetur.

“Deinde di pulchritudini mortalium se immiscuerunt.”

Nihil amplius dicit.

— III —

Postero mane Perseus proficiscitur; Iosephus una cum eo.

Athena ei veniam non dederat.

Iosephus veniam non petierat.

Longa itinera amicitiam maturant.

Cum duo homines iuxta eundem ignem dormiunt, eadem aqua bibunt, alterque hosti tergum sine timore vertit quia alter vigilat, vinculum sanguine antiquius nascitur.

Perseus pugnare novit.

Iosephus exspectare.

Perseus impetum facit.

Iosephus duo vestigia ultra videt.

Alter alterius defectum complet.

Postea homines hos duos viros aliis in terris, aliis temporibus, aliis nominibus narrabunt.

Homines enim fabulas mutant.

Sed fabulam duorum amicorum qui se invenerunt haud facile mutant.

— IV —

Cum ad Gorgonum sedes veniunt, nox iam cecidit.

Stheno dormit.

Euryale dormit.

Inter eas Medusa iacet.

Perseus primum in scutum respicit.

Tum spiritus ei deficit.

Nam quod in imagine videt monstrum non est.

Femina est adhuc intra monstrum abscondita.

Vultus inter serpentes apparens…

Fessus.

Tristis.

Innocens.

Et pulcher.

Perseus diu non movetur.

Iosephus susurrat:

“Nunc et tu amorem vidisti…”

Perseus nihil respondet.

Quarundam sententiarum responsum amor est.

Amor autem saepe tacetur.

Medusa oculos aperit.

Perseus instinctu post scutum se recipit.

Iosephus non recedit.

Oculos claudit.

“Iosephe?”

Vox Medusae e nocte venit.

Si quis vocem per annos desideraverit, nomen suum ab ea voce auditum ipsum tactum esse videatur.

Iosephus in genua procumbit.

“Adsum.”

Medusa ridet.

Amarus est risus.

“Venistine me interfectum?”

“Non.”

“Vir qui tecum est venit.”

Perseus loquitur.

“Veni.”

Silentium.

“Et nunc?”

Perseus imaginem eius in scuto aspicit.

“Nunc… nescio.”

Tum primum post multos annos Medusa vere ridet.

Risus innocens.

Quod monstrum hominibus formidolosum dubitationem viri ad se necandam missi ridiculam inveniret, dis profecto non placuisset.

Sed illa nocte di non audiebant.

Aut se audire putabant.

— V —

Tres per dies colloquuntur.

Perseus Medusam scuto tantum videt.

Iosephus iuxta eam clausis oculis sedet.

Interdum Medusa inter ambos recumbit.

At hoc mirum est:

Medusa per annos timuit ne homines eam spectarent.

Nunc ante eam duo viri sunt qui eam aspicere non possunt.

Et primum in vita sua, quamvis non videatur, intellegitur.

Perseus consilium Athenae aperit.

Nisi caput eius rettulerit, iram Polydectis effugere non poterit.

Athena autem mortem Medusae cupit.

Medusa diu tacet.

Deinde:

“Ergo me occidamus,” inquit.

Iosephus surgit.

“Non.”

“Quid ex me restat, Iosephe?”

“Tu.”

“Quis me tangere potest?”

Iosephus manum porrigit.

Oculi adhuc clausi sunt.

Medusa manum suam in palmam eius ponit.

Iosephus in saxum non vertitur.

Maledictio enim in visu est.

Non in tactu.

Medusa flere incipit.

Fortasse hoc Athena oblita erat.

Vultum hominis maledicere non satis est ut eum amore privet.

Homines tantum amorem nimis saepe in oculis quaerunt.

— VI —

Consilium a Iosepho nascitur.

Fortasse Athena nimis bene eum docuit:

Bellum ante strictum gladium vincere.

“Perseus caput Medusae amputabit.”

Perseus eum aspicit.

“Haecne pessima pars consilii est?”

“Non,” inquit Iosephus.

“Pessima postea venit.”

Veteres rumores de sanguine Gorgonis narrat.

Duplex sanguinis Gorgonei natura est.

Una vita exstinguit.

Altera vitam revocat.

Venenum et medicina eodem corpore fluunt.

Mors et vita duo cubicula eiusdem cordis sunt.

“Quid si pars falsa fluat?” rogat Perseus.

Iosephus, Medusam non intuens, subridet.

“Tum unus nostrum morietur.”

Perseus quid significet intellegit.

“Non.”

Iosephus tacet.

Medusa quoque intellegit.

“Non.”

Iosephus iterum tacet.

Nam cum homo semel se ipsum sacrificare statuit, reclamationes aliorum saepe nihil faciunt nisi valedictionem longiorem.

— VII —

Post illos dies Medusa duobus liberis gravida fit.

Genealogiae deorum de hac re mentientur.

Utriusque patrem Poseidonem esse scribent.

Templa hoc repetent.

Poetae repetent.

Sacerdotes repetent.

Et sicut omne mendacium diu repetitum, posteris fabula fiet.

Sed Medusa duos viros amat.

Duos liberos gerit.

Pegasus sanguinem Persei fert.

Chrysaor sanguinem Iosephi.

In uno utero duorum patrum liberos ferri mortalibus mirum videri potest.

Sed Medusa iam non tantum mortalis est.

Corpora a dis maledicta legibus deorum parere non necesse habent.

— VIII —

Dum sorores Gorgones dormiunt, nox iterum cadit.

Hoc tempore Medusa vere dormit.

Iosephus potionem ei dat.

“Dolebitne?” rogat Medusa.

Perseus respondere non potest.

Iosephus:

“Non memineris,” inquit.

Medusa ad vocem Iosephi se vertit.

“Tu autem?”

Iosephus subridet.

“Ego meminero.”

Perseus scutum splendidum tollit.

Non vultum Medusae, sed imaginem aspicit.

Falx curva quam manu tenet lunare lumen rapit.

Et quod historia scriptura est fit.

Perseus caput Medusae amputat.

E collo reciso duo liberi exeunt.

Pegasus in caelum ascendit.

Chrysaor e sanguine nascitur.

Eodem momento duo sanguinis rivi fluunt.

Iosephus ad venenatam partem stat.

Perseus ad salutarem.

Perseus clamat.

Corpus Medusae contremiscit.

Iosephus cadit.

Perseus eum suscipit.

Iosephus subridet.

“Athena hoc me docuit.”

“Quid?”

“In bello pretiosissimum interdum ipsum se sacrificat.”

Et moritur.

Filius Athenae, arte ab ipsa Athena edoctus, se interficiendo Athenam primum vicit.

— IX —

Perseus caput Medusae aufert.

Athena se vicisse credit.

Caput Aegidi suae imponit.

Hostibus ostendit.

Reges in saxa vertit.

Exercitus tacere cogit.

In templis homines dicunt:

“Medusa mortua est.”

At Medusa videt.

Audit.

Meminit.

Loqui non potest.

Anni transeunt.

Homines ante eam ambulant.

Alii metu aspiciunt.

Alii admiratione.

Alii nihil praeter potentiam Athenae vident.

Nemo scit oculos in Aegide adhuc vivere.

Femina quae olim propter corpus suum punita est nunc ad solum vultum redacta est.

Et ne ille quidem vultus iam suus est.

Athena eam velut telum gerit.

Medusa per annos unum tantum discit:

Exspectare.

— X —

Chrysaor crescit.

Nomen patris nescit.

Sed fabulam matris novit.

Perseus de Iosepho ei narrat.

Scutum splendidum tradit.

“Pater tuus hoc fecisset,” inquit.

“Quid?”

“Antequam deo occurreret, scivisset quomodo vinceret.”

Chrysaor templum Athenae intrat.

Athena, ubi eum videt, oculos matris agnoscit.

“Cur venisti?”

Chrysaor subridet.

“Ut te viderem.”

“Me provocas?”

“Non.”

Athena non provocationem maxime odit.

Contemptum odit.

Chrysaor hoc scit.

“Mater mea pulchrior te erat,” inquit.

Vultus Athenae mutatur.

“Quid dixisti?”

“Ne Iosephus quidem te elegit.”

Hic primum lapis finditur.

Nam ira deorum saepe non e potentia nascitur, sed e vulneribus.

Athena Aegidem tollit.

Vultum Medusae ad Chrysaorem vertit.

“Aspice matrem tuam.”

Chrysaor scutum tollit.

Sed illo momento aliud fit.

Medusa filium suum videt.

Primum post multos annos una lacrima ex oculis diu immobilibus labitur.

Primum post multos annos Medusa ipsa eligit quem in saxum vertat.

Aspectus in nitidam scuti faciem incidit.

Atque Athena oculos Medusae in scuto videt.

Quid deae periculosius esse potest quam effectum sui ipsius operis conspicere?

Primum manus graves fiunt.

Deinde pedes.

Marmor per carnem introrsum ambulat.

Athena clamare conatur.

Vox lapis fit.

Fugere conatur.

Crura lapidescunt.

Oculos ab oculis Medusae in scuto relucentibus avellere non potest.

Tandem soli oculi remanent.

Et illi quoque rigescunt.

Athena in templo suo simulacrum fit.

Sed non moritur.

Conscia est.

Videt.

Audit.

Loqui non potest.

In poenam quam Medusae dederat ipsa intrat.

Etiam di interdum iustitiam tum demum intellegunt cum ipsi eam patiuntur.

— XI —

Eodem momento quo Athena in saxum vertitur, maledictio solvitur.

Serpentes in capillis Medusae contorquentur.

Singuli silent.

Squamae eorum in aurum mutantur.

In aureos capillos.

Dentes minuuntur.

Cutis mollescit.

Et caput recisum, quod per annos in Aegide vixerat, corpus suum recordatur sicut sanguis vas suum recordatur.

Caro ossa invenit.

Collum umeros.

Umeri bracchia.

Bracchia manus.

Medusa non renascitur.

Ad se redit.

Cum speculum aspicit, monstrum non videt.

Iuvenem feminam videt quae olim in templo Athenae sederat.

Sed iam eadem femina non est.

Homo enim ad vetus corpus redire potest.

Ad veterem vitam redire non potest.

Perseus senescit.

Deinde moritur.

Mortalitas non maledictio eius est.

Natura est.

Medusa eum sepelit.

Pegasus diu supra sepulcrum patris volat.

Sic Medusa discit quid sit unum virum perdere quamvis non caesus sit,

alterum vero, quamquam mortuus est, exspectare.

— XII —

Et quodam die Medusa vasculum quod occultaverat profert.

Cum Iosephus moreretur, Perseus paucas guttas sanguinis vitae restituendae servaverat.

Medusa eas labiis Iosephi admovet.

Corpus mortuum spirat.

Sed tempus non revertitur.

Iosephus in eundem mundum redire non potest.

Itaque novo nomine eget.

Homines eum cognoscunt nomine:

**Phidias.**

Sculptor qui lapidem melius quam ceteri homines intellegit.

Nimirum lapidem intellegit.

Nam per totam vitam femina quam amavit eum aspicere non poterat quin in saxum verteretur.

Et femina quae eum educavit nunc saxum est.

— XIII —

Phidias ante Athenam stat.

Dea lapidea adhuc conscia est.

Eum agnoscit.

Iosephus manum frigidae genae Athenae imponit.

“Mater.”

Oculi Athenae moveri non possunt.

Sed Iosephus scit eam intus manere.

“Optime me educavisti.”

Tum opus incipit.

Homines putant Phidiam simulacrum Athenae facere.

Errant.

Simulacrum non facit.

Iam ibi est.

Circum corpus Athenae lapideum Phidias aurum eburque texit.

Arma facit.

Vestes facit.

Galeam facit.

In ingentem deae imaginem eam componit.

Et homines per saecula dicunt:

“Athena Parthenos Phidiae.”

Nemo scit veram Athenam in medio opere captivam stare.

Unum ex maximis totius artis simulacris simulacrum omnino non est.

Carcer est.

Et Phidias nihil aliud fecit quam ianuam eius pulchram reddere.

— XIV —

Opere perfecto, Iosephus diu ante Athenam stat.

Matrem aspicit cui olim oculos subduxerat.

Athena eum aspicit.

Primum ei imperare non potest.

Primum Iosephus veniam abeundi non petit.

Terga vertit.

Domum redit.

Medusa in horto est.

Sol comis eius incidit.

Aureus color adhuc manet.

Nulli serpentes.

Nullum scutum.

Nullum speculum.

Iosephus in limine consistit.

Medusa eum aspicit.

Iosephus oculos non claudit sicut olim.

Timor in vultu Medusae apparet.

Nam quidam timores maledictione soluta non statim evanescunt.

“Iosephe…”

Iosephus ad eam accedit.

Et primum recta fronte in oculos eius aspicit.

Momentum transit.

Deinde alterum.

In saxum non vertitur.

Nihil fit.

Medusa plorat.

Iosephus manum vultui eius imponit.

Et primum in vita sua Medusa non timet quod vir eam aspicit.

Homines postea narrabunt Medusam monstrum fuisse.

Perseum eam interfecisse narrabunt.

Athenam caput eius velut telum tulisse narrabunt.

Phidiam simulacrum Athenae fecisse narrabunt.

Nihil horum omnino falsum est.

Fabulae enim raro mentiuntur.

Tantum statuunt quam partem veritatis narrent.

Et interdum, ut fabula mulieris mutetur, nihil mutandum est ex iis quae de illa dicta sunt.

Una tantum quaestio, quam nemo rogavit, roganda est:

Non—

**In quid Medusa mutata est?**

Sed—

**Quid Medusae accidit?**
